## Chapter 331: Childhood Sweethearts Meet at the Banquet

Leaving Xilan to tend Mengrong, Shen Jianchen stepped out into the courtyard with Qianli Xun and told her how it had happened.

“Who would have guessed Grandpa Mao’s lyrics took a trip back to the Song dynasty through you?” she sighed. “Even placed in this era, the old man’s verse outshines every poet alive.”

“Isn’t that the truth?”

“Can Mengrong’s illness truly not be cured?”

“I called every famed physician in Bianliang. Every single one said no.”

Just then Matron Zou entered the courtyard. Seeing the prince there lit hope in her eyes—until she saw Youqi Jushi beside him and understood at once why he had come. She stepped forward and asked for a private word with the poetess.

“We’ve finished talking anyway,” Shen Jianchen said, heading back toward his own quarters to wait—relishing, for once, the sweet torture of waiting in hope.

Once he left, Matron Zou sank to her knees. “Youqi Jushi, no matter how many wrongs Princess Consort Yi committed against you, for His Highness’s sake, please forgive her. She has paid dearly already, reduced to this witless state. His Highness no longer spares her a glance; in this whole manor, only Xilan and I still treat her like a princess consort.”

“Please get up! You are my elder—how can you kneel to me?” Helping Matron Zou rise, Qianli Xun went on, “I did resent Mengrong once, yet I’ve forgiven her now. I only hope she recovers soon so we can go back to how things used to be.”

Matron Zou dabbed her eyes. “To forgive her proves how good-hearted you are. No wonder His Highness cannot put you out of his mind. Poor Princess Consort Yi lacked the blessing and tore herself to shreds. I only wish you and His Highness joyful, harmonious days ahead.”

Qianli Xun blinked. “Why say that, Matron?”

Realizing she had slipped—Prince Yi had forbidden anyone to mention the Imperial Decree—Matron Zou hurried to cover herself. “Everyone in the manor knows His Highness holds you in his heart. Just an old woman’s fond wish, nothing more. Let me look in on the Princess Consort.”

Walking back toward her guest courtyard in a daze, Qianli Xun crossed paths with Bai Feng, who told her Prince Yi wanted her in his rooms. Thinking of Matron Zou’s words and Shen Jianchen’s tenderness, she balked—one step inside his quarters and her defenses might crumble, tangling her life all over again. Remembering Haoyu’s devotion and her own quiet dream of an ordinary life, she turned toward the guest hall. “I’m going to my room.”

“His Highness said you must come.”

“And if I refuse? What can you do to me?”

Knowing her temper well, Bai Feng pleaded. “I’m just a guard caught in the middle! If you don’t go, His Highness will scold me. Do me a favor and come!”

“What does he want?”

“An invitation arrived. That’s why he sent for you.”

Relief washed over her. “Why didn’t you say it was an invitation in the first place instead of wasting my breath?” She turned toward the prince’s courtyard while Bai Feng muttered behind her that she hadn’t asked sooner.

Inside, Shen Jianchen handed over a formal card. “Lady Wei invited both of us to a banquet at Zeng Manor tomorrow. Her name is Wei Wan, wife of Chancellor Zeng. I’ve met her a few times—a bold, frank spirit worth knowing. If you’re interested, I’ll take you tomorrow.”

Bearing Zhu Shuzhen’s name, she wouldn’t miss it for the world. “Of course I’m going! Lady Wei is a celebrated female ci poet too. We’ll have plenty to talk about.”

Lady Wei held her gathering at home, inviting a handful of cultured friends and not a single court politician—barring even her own husband, Chancellor Zeng. When the Chancellor had once objected, she ignored him flatly and went on her own way, standing apart from every noblewoman in Bianliang.

Seeing a poised young beauty walk in beside Prince Yi, Lady Wei guessed her identity at once and came forward in delight. “Prince Yi graces Zeng Manor with fresh luster!” Looking Qianli Xun up and down, she added, “Your reputation falls short of the truth! Your verses are lovely, and your looks lovelier still.”

Qianli Xun studied Wei Wan in turn: past forty, wearing no flashy gold or jade, understated yet carrying natural grace. “I have long admired Lady Wei’s name. Meeting you today is Shuzhen’s great honor.”

“Kindred spirits at first sight,” Lady Wei laughed, taking her hand. “Come, let’s walk in together.”

The banquet hall held three tables along either side and one at the head. The instant Qianli Xun stepped inside, she felt a pair of eyes fastened on her. Looking across, she met the gaze of a refined, handsome young gentleman, who nodded with a warm smile. Returning a polite smile, she wondered if the real Zhu Shuzhen knew him, wishing Kuqiao were here to whisper who he was.

Lady Wei guided her to the highest guest seat beside Prince Yi and asked the prince’s leave to sit at the head as hostess. Once seated, she raised her voice to the scholars gathered.

“Gentlemen, I hold today’s banquet for one purpose alone: to welcome Youqi Jushi. I have admired her for years and wrote inviting her north, though distance kept us apart. Three years ago I even asked Qiao Wenxian to carry my invitation urging her to visit Bianliang. Did my message reach you, Youqi Jushi?”

Searching her memory, Qianli Xun couldn’t recall Wenyuan mentioning it—he had probably forgotten. Unwilling to get him scolded, she covered smoothly. “Wenyuan did tell me, Lady Wei, and I longed to come pay my respects, yet circumstance and the long road kept me away. Meeting you today is Heaven’s grace.”

“Heaven’s grace indeed, or how else could you have escaped prison?” Lady Wei sighed. “Back in the fifth year of Xuanhe, when I heard you were locked up upon arriving in Bianliang, I forced Chancellor Zeng to plead with His Majesty on your behalf. Yet a grand chancellor of the realm feared a certain person, and as a woman, my hands were tied.” She looked toward Shen Jianchen. “Only when I heard Prince Yi was running himself ragged to save you did I breathe easier.”

Deeply moved, Qianli Xun raised her cup. “Though we had never met, you tried to save me from danger. Accept this cup in gratitude.”

Lady Wei raised hers in reply. “No thanks needed—I accomplished nothing. Let us all raise our cups to Youqi Jushi’s deliverance from an undeserved calamity!”

Everyone drank together. Setting her cup down, Lady Wei clapped her hands, and a troupe of dancing girls glided in to perform.

Shen Jianchen spared the dancers no glance; his eyes locked onto the young gentleman across the hall staring shamelessly at Qianli Xun. Already planning how to teach the fellow a lesson afterward, he leaned close and whispered, “Why is that man staring straight at you? Do you know him?”

Absorbed in the dance, Qianli Xun looked across just as the man lifted his cup in a silent toast. Awkwardly, she lifted hers in a brief nod and set it back down without drinking.

“I asked you a question,” Shen Jianchen pressed.

“I don’t know him,” she whispered back.

“If you don’t know him, why pay him any mind?”

“He toasted me. It’s rude not to nod back.”

“He’s up to no good. Ignore him.”

“Fine, I’ll listen to you.” She barely knew why she gave in so easily—perhaps touched by everything he had done, perhaps swayed by lingering feeling. Catching herself, she thought of Haoyu at once. “Still, I wonder what day Chen Haoyu will reach Bianliang.”

That single question snuffed out the warmth glowing in Shen Jianchen’s chest. He longed to tell her Haoyu no longer mattered, that the decree had already ended their marriage—yet stayed silent, bound by his pledge and unwilling to accept a reluctant bride. His smile died. Reading his sudden chill, she knew her words had stung, though she had only said them to remind herself she was a married woman.

When the dancers withdrew, Lady Wei beamed. “For your first visit to my banquet, Youqi Jushi, you cannot leave empty-handed. Leave us a gem of verse.”

Prepared for this, Qianli Xun rose gracefully. “No gem, yet in gratitude for your hospitality, Shuzhen will gladly wield the brush and risk making a fool of herself.”

Servants brought a table, spread fine paper, and arranged brush and ink. Step by step, Qianli Xun composed five quatrains on the spot:

Poem I

When pipes and strings urge her onto the brocade carpet,

Her figure is light as if ready to fly.

Had Emperor Minghuang seen her on that day,

Even Aman could have offered no match, let alone Consort Yang.

Poem II

The fragrant cushion holds steady her half-crescent slippers;

Back and forth she skims the waves till cloud-shadows fade.

As strings quicken the beat to seize every measure,

Both sleeves whirl round like drifting snow.

Poem III

Her willow waist refuses spring’s restraint;

Turning in the wind like a phoenix, rosy-cloud sleeves wheel round.

Dancing through “Yizhou” till her strength can hold no more,

Before the banquet mat fallen blossoms scatter thick and full.

Poem IV

Claiming all of the capital’s foremost spring,

Her clear songs and wondrous dances rise above the crowd.

I only grieve that at dawn the guests will scatter like stars,

And she will vanish like a cloud over Mount Wu.

Poem V

Amid the candlelight her powdered grace rests calm,

Yet one touch of sorrow steals into the twin hills of her brows.

Fearless of carrying it past the swallow incense burner,

In silence they follow each other, mindful of her arched slippers.

*At Lady Wei’s Banquet* [TN: Translator’s rendering.]

As she finished each poem, servants carried the sheet to Lady Wei, who passed it round to warm praise. At the fourth poem, a guest exclaimed aloud over the closing couplet, praising how it captured both the guests’ reluctance to part and the dancer’s haunting grace.

After Qianli Xun returned to her seat, the young gentleman across the hall spoke up with Lady Wei’s leave—who addressed him as Young Master Xu.

That name jolted Qianli Xun’s memory: right after transmigrating, Kuqiao had told her about Zhu Shuzhen’s first love, a Young Master Xu! That explained every glance across the table. Listening with sharp ears, she watched Zhu Shuzhen’s former sweetheart speak.

“These five poems fit the evening well,” Young Master Xu said, “yet I prize her *Circle Ci* above all—not a single written character on the page, yet steeped in romantic grace.”

Every guest agreed, marveling that expressing longing through circles and dots had neither precedent before nor equal after. Qianli Xun wanted to tell them the Emperor had forced it out of her, yet held back since Zhu Shuzhen hadn’t written it under duress in history, realizing all the more how she was walking Shuzhen’s fated path.

“If memory serves, Young Master Xu’s family came from Lin’an too?” Lady Wei asked.

“Yes,” Xu replied. “Our Xu family lived in Lin’an, and Youqi Jushi and I grew up together. The Xu and Zhu families stood on the closest terms.”

Lady Wei turned in surprise to ask if it were true.

Now certain this was the very man whose family moved away on a geomancer’s nonsense—leaving Zhu Shuzhen waiting in vain until age forced her into marriage with Lin Jue—Qianli Xun masked her disdain behind cool courtesy. “You may not know, Lady Wei: I suffered a bout of amnesia once and remember nothing of earlier years, so I cannot tell whether Young Master Xu speaks truth or fable.”

Disappointment flickered across Young Master Xu’s face, clear he thought she merely refused to claim him. Even better, she thought—let him understand he meant nothing to Zhu Shuzhen now. For the rest of the feast, he drank sullenly alone without looking her way again.

More music, dance, and verses followed. As the banquet ended, Lady Wei invited Prince Yi and Youqi Jushi into an inner room, standing instead of asking them to sit.

“I called you both aside for Princess Consort Yi,” Lady Wei said. “I heard of her sickness, and indeed nobody in Bianliang can cure it. Last year in Yanjing, though, I saw a patient’s family kowtow in gratitude on the street after a miracle physician cured the exact same illness. Why not take Princess Consort Yi to Yanjing and seek him out?”
