## Chapter 330: Mid-Autumn of the Seventh Year

After his beating, Liang Shicheng suspected Prince Yi at once, yet without a shred of proof he had to swallow the humiliation in silence.

Back in Lin’an, Chen Haoyu called the Chen and Zhu families together, explained his wife’s situation in Bianliang, and read out the Imperial Decree. Alarmed, everyone asked what he meant to do.

Haoyu laid out his plan and produced Prince Yi’s signed pledge: dissolving the marriage was only a stopgap for show, while in truth he would wait for her return. Having no better choice, both families agreed.

They summoned a matchmaker, signed papers dissolving the betrothal without dividing any property, and let her submit the document to the local yamen. On paper, the engagement stood void; inside both households, nothing changed—not a single curtain in Mo Xiang Courtyard was touched.

Later, families repeatedly sent matchmakers to propose new brides for Haoyu, and the Chen household turned every one away. Haoyu threw himself into the family business and ignored women altogether, winning Zhu Xiyan’s deep respect. Once Zhu Xiyan even urged him to take a concubine; Haoyu refused flatly, swearing to wait for his wife.

His happiest days came every half-year when he traveled to Bianliang to visit her in prison. Seeing her moved to a much better cell—clearly Prince Yi’s doing—he watched her words closely and went home relieved each time that she still knew nothing of the decree.

Months crept past until the seventh year of Xuanhe arrived. With the Mid-Autumn Festival drawing near, Haoyu skipped family reunion plans, readied a carriage, and set out north with Kuqiao and Qinggua. Nearly two years since she last saw Bai Feng, Kuqiao hoped not only to bring her mistress home, but also to talk marriage so their own plans wouldn’t drag on forever.

Just outside Jiangning Prefecture, on an open stretch of road, bandits armed with broadswords and heavy hammers leaped out and surrounded the carriage, glaring like fiends.

Seeing escape blocked, Haoyu stepped down with a forced smile. “We’re just ordinary travelers passing through. Brave heroes like you rob the rich to aid the poor—there’s no cause to trouble common folk like us.”

“Nice carriage, fine silk on your back,” the bandit leader sneered. “You reek of money, and your kind is exactly who we rob. Hand over your wealth and I’ll spare your neck.”

“Big Brother, keep the people too!” another bandit called. “Our kitchen cook ran off today—the driver and those two fellows will fill in nicely. And there’s a woman inside the carriage we can gift to Little Brother!”

Listening inside, Kuqiao shivered in terror.

Knowing talk couldn’t save them all, Haoyu played along. “I’ll get every valuable out right now.” Turning to the carriage, he barked loudly for Qinggua to bring down all the silver, while muttering low to the driver: once Qinggua climbed down and they opened the chest, spook the horse and race Kuqiao clear of danger.

The driver kept quiet, slipped a hand into his coat, and drew out a long sewing needle he carried for mending torn clothes—never dreaming it would save lives today.

When Qinggua carried the wooden chest down, Haoyu caught the driver’s eye and told the bandit leader his servant would open the chest of silver notes for inspection. As every bandit’s gaze locked onto the lid, the driver jabbed the needle deep into the horse’s rump. Screaming in pain, the horse bolted ahead in a frenzy.

Stunned bandits scattered out of its path, watching helplessly as the carriage tore down the road with Kuqiao inside.

Regaining their wits, a bandit pressed a broadsword to Haoyu’s throat. “Sly bastard! You let the girl get away!”

“Don’t jump to conclusions, gentlemen!” Haoyu raised his hands. “How would I know why the horse bolted? A carriage and one maid aren’t worth a copper—all our silver notes are right here!”

Qinggua flipped the lid. Seeing a full chest of silver notes, the bandits forgot the runaway maid at once, and the leader ordered men, servant, and silver hauled back to their mountain stronghold.

Haoyu cared little for his own skin; what terrified him was missing the prison release and letting Prince Yi tell her about the decree. Keeping his head, he waited for a chance to slip away with Qinggua.

Miles down the road, the winded horse came to a halt. Shaken half to death, Kuqiao saw they were unharmed and panicked over her young master.

“Our only move is to race back to Lin’an and fetch help,” the driver told her.

“Aren’t we going to Bianliang to meet my miss?”

“Prince Yi is there for Young Madam. Saving Young Master comes first.”

Seeing the sense in it, Kuqiao agreed, though she dreaded taking the same road back into the bandits’ lap.

“Years ago this road was safe,” the driver sighed. “Seeing bandits even here shows folks have been driven out of honest livings. Don’t fret—there are plenty of back roads to Lin’an.”

Mid-Autumn arrived in Bianliang. Haoyu never showed up—which suited Shen Jianchen just fine. Standing outside the prison gates, heart pounding after nearly two years of waiting, he watched Qianli Xun step out into the open air. Squinting against daylight after so long indoors, she adjusted after a moment, looked around, and saw no sign of Haoyu. Doubtless delayed on the road, she thought.

Shen Jianchen stepped forward, gentle yet commanding. “Get in the carriage. Come back to Prince Yi’s Manor with me.”

She climbed inside without a word of protest. During his prison visits they had talked effortlessly; now sitting face to face in freedom left both unsure how to begin.

Shen Jianchen only sat smiling at her. For the first time she shared a carriage with him willingly—and under His Majesty’s decree, she already belonged to him.

Blushing under his gaze, she asked, “Why keep staring at me?”

“Because I like looking at you. What are you going to do about it?”

Too playful to argue with, she turned toward the window with a wry smile—then the smile faded. “Why isn’t Chen Haoyu here? Didn’t he come to Bianliang?”

Seeing she still worried over Haoyu, Shen Jianchen looked away. “He promised to come fetch you. Something on the road must have held him up. Give it a few more days; he’ll show up.”

She asked no more, and both rode on lost in their thoughts.

At Prince Yi’s Manor, maids helped Qianli Xun wash away nearly two years of prison grime, dress in fresh robes, pin up her hair, and touch her cheeks with powder until she shone anew. Then the kitchen spread a feast in the dining hall, and Shen Jianchen dismissed every servant so they sat alone.

“Prison food is miserable,” he said. “Eat your fill today.”

“Trying to stuff me to death?”

“The spread shows my heart. Eat as much as you like.”

Dispensing with manners, Qianli Xun grabbed a roast chicken drumstick and tore right in.

“Not one trace of a highborn lady’s grace,” Shen Jianchen teased with a laugh.

“I just walked out of a prison cell. Highborn grace went out the window long ago.”

His smile vanished. “Don’t ever call yourself that. In my eyes, you were never a criminal.”

“Thank you for seeing me that way.” She went right back to chewing while he picked up his chopsticks to join her.

Not until she finished the chicken leg, three bowls of assorted-flavor broth, and half a dish of braised cabbage and declared herself full did Shen Jianchen set down his chopsticks in earnest.

“I have something to ask you.”

“Speak.”

“You have two choices right now: go back to Lin’an with Chen Haoyu, or stay in Bianliang with me. Which do you choose?”

She let out a soft burp, caught off guard, and laughed it off. “Isn’t that obvious nonsense? I’m Chen Haoyu’s wife.”

“If there were no marriage contract—if you stood completely free—which would you pick?”

Remembering Haoyu asking the exact same question in her cell, she wondered what had gotten into both men. “Stop asking silly questions. There’s no such luck.”

She had already resolved to build a life with Haoyu—after kissing him in prison, turning back felt wrong. Shen Jianchen’s devotion tore at her heart, yet reality left her only one path. “I know how much you’ve done for me. That debt can only be repaid in our next life.”

“I don’t want another life. I want this life—sharing the same roof with you.”

Panic fluttered in her chest. Stay one minute longer and her resolve might buckle, turning her into the unfaithful wretch people loved to condemn. “I… I haven’t seen Mengrong since coming out. I’ll go see her now!” She fled the room in haste.

Shen Jianchen let out a long breath. He burned to tell her about the decree, yet kept his word to Haoyu. Nor did he want to force her hand with an imperial scroll; he wanted her heart freely given. Rising, he followed after her—breaking his own vow never to visit Mengrong’s courtyard only because Qianli Xun walked there.

Out in the yard, Xilan brightened at the sight of the prince, until realizing he only trailed behind Youqi Jushi.

Inside, Mengrong reclined in a rattan chair, cradling a crafted silk plum blossom and murmuring to herself. “This is the plum blossom Brother loves. He says I look just like one.”

Qianli Xun sat beside her. “Mengrong, do you still know me?”

Mengrong blinked. “Shuzhen?”

“Yes, it’s me!” Qianli Xun nodded eagerly.

Dropping the silk flower, Mengrong grabbed her hand and hauled her straight to the bed. “Lie down. Let’s lie down together.”

Shen Jianchen watched bewildered as both women stretched out side by side without even taking off their shoes. After a minute, Mengrong announced day had broken and they should sleep in together, and Qianli Xun played along. Then Mengrong bolted upright shouting for a brazier.

Xilan carried in a cold brazier without charcoal, set down two stools, and Mengrong dragged Qianli Xun beside it, babbling about sitting down for a chat. Tears slipped down Qianli Xun’s cheeks. Broken as Mengrong’s mind was, the only memories left in it were their happiest girlhood days. In that instant, every scrap of resentment vanished; truth be told, she had never truly blamed Mengrong, knowing she herself had loved Mengrong’s brother in secret.

Suddenly Mengrong gripped her hand tight. “Shuzhen, promise you won’t steal my brother, all right?”

Tears fell faster. “I won’t steal him. Never.”

Mengrong beamed, then tilted her head toward the ceiling. “Too bad my brother went up to heaven. You’ll never find him. One day I’ll go join him too. He loves me alone—me alone forever.”

Pained for her mistress, Xilan stepped up and announced that Prince Yi had come to visit. Spotting a figure in the doorway, Mengrong leaped up and threw her arms around him. “Brother? Did you come down from heaven to see me? Have you come to take me up with you?”

Shen Jianchen stood still without pushing her away. A heartbeat later, Mengrong shoved him back hard. “You’re not my brother! You’re not! Where’s my plum blossom? Give me my plum blossom!”

Xilan picked up the silk flower and put it in her arms. Mengrong hugged it like a treasure and began reciting:

“Wind and rain escorted Spring’s departure,

Flying snow welcomes Spring’s return.

On the ice-clad rock rising high and sheer,

A flower blooms sweet and fair.

Sweet and fair, she craves not Spring for herself alone,

To be the harbinger of Spring she is content.

When the mountain flowers are in full bloom,

She will smile mingling in their midst.” [TN: Translated by the Foreign Languages Press.]

Hearing the verse, Shen Jianchen froze in astonishment. Even lost in madness, Mengrong remembered the poem he had borrowed on the day he arrived in this body.

Stunned herself, Qianli Xun shot Shen Jianchen a questioning look. “What on earth is going on?”

“It’s a long story,” he replied. “I’ll tell you in a moment.”
