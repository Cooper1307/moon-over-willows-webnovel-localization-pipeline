## Chapter 328: Kneeling Until Dawn for Her Life

Two full days passed with no word of Prince Yi returning. Back at the manor, panic spread. Between tending Princess Consort Yi, Matron Zou and Xilan hurried to the front courtyard hour after hour for news.

Unable to sit still, Chen Haoyu and Bai Feng rode to Xihua Gate. When they asked the guards inside, they learned Prince Yi had been kneeling outside Zichen Hall for two days straight.

“Let us inside!” they pleaded.

“What do you take the imperial palace for?” a guard snapped. “Think anyone can wander in?”

Bai Feng flashed the waist token. “I’m Prince Yi’s personal guard. Surely I can go in?”

“Two days ago you came for the imperial physicians, who sit in the outer court,” the guard replied. “Today is different. Prince Yi is kneeling outside Zichen Hall in the inner palace. With neither rank nor office, neither of you may step inside.”

Bai Feng tucked the token away and tried bluffing that he needed a physician again, only for the guard to advise him against wasting breath: even past the outer gate, another watch guarded the inner palace. As an imperial son, Prince Yi wouldn’t come to serious harm anyway.

When Haoyu moved to rush past, the guards drew their swords, forcing him back. Seeing no way in, Bai Feng offered to go wait at the manor, yet Haoyu refused to leave. Scanning the open ground outside Xihua Gate, Haoyu dropped to his knees as well—if Prince Yi could kneel, so could he. Sighing at his stubbornness, Bai Feng promised to send Qinggua back with a warm cloak, secretly sizing up the palace wall to confirm he could vault over it once night fell.

For two days and nights outside Zichen Hall, Shen Jianchen neither ate a grain of rice nor drank a drop of water. By day the chill was bearable, yet late-autumn night winds cut to the bone until his whole body shook and his knees went dead numb. Still, no sign came from His Majesty or Liang Shicheng. Doubt crept in, yet knowing no other path remained, he held firm.

As dusk deepened into dark on the second night, a gust set him shivering—and then a warm cloak settled over his shoulders. Turning his head, he found Bai Feng crouched beside him.

“How did you get in here?”

Seeing his master’s gaunt face and cracked, peeling lips, Bai Feng unhooked a waterskin. “Hot water, Your Highness. Still warm from the ride. Drink up.”

Shen Jianchen gulped down every drop before sighing in relief. Whispering that he had slipped over the wall and couldn’t stay long, Bai Feng pressed a bundle of pastries into his hands for when nobody looked.

Shen Jianchen pushed the pastries back, handed over the empty waterskin, and pulled off the cloak. “Take them back. If anyone catches me warm and fed, all this kneeling goes to waste. Hurry before you’re spotted.”

With no choice, Bai Feng gathered the things. “If you won’t take them, I’ll bring them to Young Master Chen kneeling outside the gate.”

“Chen Haoyu is kneeling outside?”

“Yes. Just as foolish as Your Highness.”

Hearing boots approaching, Shen Jianchen waved him off. Bai Feng vanished into the night air via light-foot skill an instant before the patrol passed. Spotting nothing amiss, the guards only glanced at the prince in quiet awe at his endurance.

Outside Xihua Gate, Qinggua hovered anxiously over Haoyu. “What good does kneeling out here do, Young Master? Come back with me.”

“If my wife makes it out alive, at least she’ll know I bore a share of this too,” Haoyu muttered.

“Why compete with Prince Yi over who can suffer more? She’s already married to you. No matter how much he endures, once she’s freed she goes back to Lin’an with you.”

“True enough, yet I dread she’ll find it even harder to let him go.”

Bai Feng materialized silently beside them and dropped the cloth bag of pastries into Haoyu’s lap. “His Highness refused to eat them. They’re yours.” He handed Qinggua the extra cloak for the night cold.

Learning that Prince Yi had touched only the water, Haoyu shoved the pastries back to Qinggua and shrugged off the cloak Qinggua had wrapped around him earlier. “If Prince Yi won’t take them, neither will I! Take everything back.”

“Prince Yi is putting on a show for His Majesty,” Qinggua argued. “Nobody inside can even see you! Why torment yourself?”

“I’m putting on a show for my own conscience. It’s the only way my heart rests easy.”

“Who ever heard of putting on a show for yourself?”

“Save your breath. Go back with Bai Feng and stop bothering me.”

Refusing to leave his master, Qinggua stayed—and Bai Feng stayed too, sleeping in the carriage through the night for Prince Yi’s sake.

At first light, officials riding into Xihua Gate for morning court stared at Haoyu kneeling on the stones, and stared again when leaving after court adjourned. Some sighed, some snickered, and some urged him to go home since no power could overturn a closed sentence from the Court of Judicial Review.

Inside Zichen Hall, Zhao Ji never expected Prince Yi’s resolve to hold through three days without food or drink. Fearing the boy’s life might actually slip away, he sent a junior attendant outside with water and food.

Head drooping and strength fading, Shen Jianchen forced his eyes open when the attendant spoke. “Tell His Majesty this: unless Youqi Jushi’s life is spared, I will kneel until my dying breath, touching neither food nor drink.”

Hearing the exact message, Zhao Ji summoned Liang Shicheng.

“Your Majesty sent for me over Prince Yi?” Liang asked, bowing.

Zhao Ji smiled. “You know me best. He is my own son after all. Three days now—if he keeps kneeling like this, his life may be in real danger.”

Liang read his master at once. Favorite though he was, he remained a servant, whereas the man outside was blood of the Emperor’s loins. Letting the prince kneel three days unheeded had already honored Liang’s face enough. “My grievance is trifling; His Highness’s safety comes first. Whatever Your Majesty decides, I obey without complaint.”

Rising to pat Liang’s shoulder, Zhao Ji said, “Since you came through unscathed, why not spare her death? I promise you: spare her life, yet you decide what living punishment she endures.”

Liang seized the opening. “Then lock her in prison for a few years, to ease the outrage in my heart.”

“Good. Approved.”

Still eager to stir the pot, Liang added smoothly, “Has Your Majesty wondered why Prince Yi risks death kneeling outside your hall for a poetess?”

“Why?”

“When I visited Lin’an, I learned Prince Yi adores her—even crashed her wedding day, till the whole city knew of it.”

“Such a thing happened?”

“What is more, the imperial physicians report Princess Consort Yi has lost her wits beyond all cure. A prince needs someone beside him. Why not bestow this poetess upon Prince Yi as a secondary consort?”

“A fine idea! At least his days on those stones won’t go unrewarded. Still, we cannot cite his stubbornness as our reason for pardoning a capital crime. We need a fitting pretext.”

Liang had two ready. “First, declare that since I escaped fatal harm, I chose magnanimity and asked only that she repent in prison. Second, our dynasty treasures men and women of letters; out of love for rare talent, Your Majesty spares her.”

“Excellent! Have her compose a ci poem too, so I have grounds to praise her gift.” Returning to his desk, Zhao Ji reflected, “I have read her verses before—delicate and wistful in plenty, though lacking heroic sweep. Still, for a woman of the inner chambers, one cannot ask too much. Her depth falls slightly short, yet her craft is peerless—even I feel humbled beside it. Trouble yourself to send someone to the prison and tell her: if she can compose a lyric without a single written word that still makes me slap the table in amazement, I will spare her life.”

“Your servant obeys.”

Stepping out of Zichen Hall, Liang bowed with mock humility before Shen Jianchen. “Get up and go home, Your Highness. What use do I have for the poetess’s life? Since you fancy her, she is yours.”

Barely trusting his ears, Shen Jianchen lifted his exhausted face. “Are you telling the truth?”

“Would I dare deceive Your Highness? His Majesty agreed to spare her and is sending someone to fetch a poem from her.”

Relief cut the last thread holding him upright; Shen Jianchen slumped onto the flagstones. Liang waved two guards over to carry him out to his waiting carriage.

Seeing Prince Yi emerge, Bai Feng ran up to take over from the guards. Haoyu scrambled to his feet too, only for his sore, cramped legs to give way until Qinggua caught him.

“Did you save my wife?!” Haoyu gasped, breathless.

Too starved to speak, Shen Jianchen stayed silent. Qinggua helped his young master onto a roadside stone, rolled up his trousers over raw knees, and reached for a bottle of salve he had brought along.

“Leave mine for a moment,” Haoyu insisted. “Put it on Prince Yi first. His knees must be twice as bad.”

After quickly tending Haoyu’s knees, Qinggua passed the bottle through the carriage window to Bai Feng, who set to work on the prince. Leaning back against the cushions, Shen Jianchen drained his waterskin, relishing the simple bliss of sitting upright again, then asked for food.

Bai Feng passed over the bag of pastries, beaming that Youqi Jushi must be safe. Shen Jianchen bolted down pastries that he barely touched on normal days, felt his strength return, and ordered the driver home.

As the carriage rolled, Haoyu climbed aboard and asked again, “Is she really saved?”

“Would I have left the palace if she weren’t?”

Haoyu burst into loud sobs, then laughed through his tears as Shen Jianchen smiled weakly and told him he looked hideous crying.

Meanwhile in prison, two junior attendants set brush and paper on the low wooden table in Qianli Xun’s cell, relayed the Emperor’s command, and urged her to write fast.

Resigned to death three days ago, Qianli Xun felt joy surge through her chest. Doubtless Prince Yi had moved mountains for her. Better yet, the Emperor’s impossible riddle suited her perfectly: Zhu Shuzhen actually had a famous piece built from nothing but circles and dots—written for longing originally, now pressed into service to save her neck.

Dipping the brush, she filled the sheet with circles and black dots. The two attendants gaped, bewildered by how anyone could compose verse without words, thinking the poor poetess reduced to scribbling nonsense.

Handing the sheet over, Qianli Xun asked softly how Prince Yi fared. One attendant told her Prince Yi had knelt outside Zichen Hall for nearly three days until His Majesty relented.

Hearing that, Qianli Xun sighed quietly inside. Her debt to Shen Jianchen grew deeper by the day—a debt she could never clear in this life, only pray to repay in the next, when nothing would stand between them.
